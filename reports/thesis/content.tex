\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Context and motivation}

Weather affects both driving conditions and the decision to travel. Strong
wind can make a vehicle harder to control, low temperature can coincide with
slippery roads, and darkness can reduce visibility. These conditions are
relevant to Icelandic roads, where terrain can produce substantial local
variation in wind \citep{snaebjornsson2007,pu2018}. Precipitation and fog are
also relevant road-weather hazards, but they are context for this study and
are not analysed here.

Previous research provides two reasons to examine wind. Engineering studies
describe mechanisms by which crosswind can destabilise vehicles, while
observational studies compare accident occurrence with the frequency of windy
conditions \citep{baker1986,baker1994risk,edwards1994}. These are complementary
forms of evidence: a physical mechanism does not establish how often accidents
occur across a road network. Reviews also find less consistent road-safety
evidence for wind than for precipitation \citep{theofilatos2014}. Local
evidence is therefore needed to determine whether the same pattern is present
on Icelandic roads.

In Iceland, wind-related road safety has been studied using vehicle-reliability
analyses and operational vehicle-wind models
\citep{snaebjornsson2007,hrafnkelsdottir2020}. This thesis addresses a
different question: whether rural injury accidents are over-represented under
particular weather conditions relative to how often those conditions occur
locally. It then examines how the interpretation changes when available traffic
information is introduced. The study therefore links a long Icelandic accident
series with local weather frequency and two complementary traffic-exposure
approaches, while treating station observations as proxies for road conditions
rather than direct measurements of the forces acting on a vehicle.

The study covers rural injury accidents in Iceland from 2007--2025. Accident
times and locations are linked to nearby 10-minute weather observations. Mean
wind is the primary weather measure; wind gust and temperature are secondary
measures, while season, time of day and daylight provide additional context.

Accident counts alone do not measure accident risk. Severe weather may increase
the hazard faced by each vehicle while also reducing the number of vehicles on
the road. Weather frequency and traffic volume are therefore considered
separately \citep{andrey2003,lord2010,washington2020}.

\section{Aim and analysis strategy}

The primary research question is: \emph{How does rural injury accident
occurrence vary with weather, particularly strong wind, after accounting as
far as possible for local weather frequency and traffic exposure?}

Three steps guide the analysis:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Weather-frequency O/E analysis.} Compare observed accidents
  with the number expected from local weather frequency, by parameter and season.
  This measures over-representation relative to weather time, not risk per vehicle.
  \item \textbf{Approximate traffic-corrected O/E analysis.} Establish how traffic
  changes with weather, then use the observed counter response to reweight the
  baseline comparison. This asks whether weather-related changes in traffic explain the observed weather patterns, particularly the high-wind pattern.
  \item \textbf{Vehicle-kilometres travelled (VKT) analysis.} Relate accident counts to
  estimated vehicle-kilometres using daily traffic, rural road length and monthly
  weather frequencies, including eligible accident-free counter-days.
\end{enumerate}

The three analyses use different denominators and, for the VKT analysis, a different study sample. The baseline and approximate correction cover 2007--2025, whereas the
VKT analysis uses a smaller 2019--2024 sample. The study estimates associations, not causal effects
or a complete national accident rate per vehicle-kilometre.

\section{Thesis outline}

Chapter 2 gives the background for the weather and traffic comparisons.
Chapter 3 describes the data, data preparation, and statistical methods.
Chapter 4 presents the three main analyses in the order of these steps, followed
by interpretation of their differences. Chapter 5 discusses their interpretation and limitations,
and Chapter 6 gives the conclusions.

\chapter{Background}

\section{Wind and rural road accidents}

Crosswind can produce overturning, lateral sideslip, or excessive yaw. The
critical condition depends on vehicle geometry and mass, vehicle speed, road
friction, wind direction, and driver response rather than wind speed alone
\citep{baker1986,baker1994risk}. High-sided vehicles are particularly exposed,
but the physical mechanisms also provide a reason to examine loss-of-control
accidents more generally. These engineering studies establish physical
plausibility and vehicle-specific failure mechanisms; they do not by
themselves establish a population-level association between wind and accident
occurrence.

This problem is directly relevant in Iceland, where terrain can accelerate and
redirect near-surface wind over short distances. An Icelandic reliability
study by \citet{snaebjornsson2007} modelled accident probability as a combined
function of wind velocity and direction, road camber and friction, and vehicle
speed.
A station measures wind at one point, whereas vehicle stability also depends
on the wind's direction relative to the road and on local road conditions.
Terrain between a station and a road can alter the wind reaching the vehicle.
Station observations therefore remain exposure proxies, and a shorter
matching distance does not guarantee identical conditions. Mobile road
measurement research similarly shows that roadside terrain creates marked
spatial variation that a sparse fixed-station network cannot fully resolve
\citep{pu2018}.

Vegagerðin has developed a vehicle-wind model to support decisions about
hazardous conditions and road closures, while also noting the limited
experimental evidence available for passenger cars \citep{hrafnkelsdottir2020}. The present thesis does not test that
operational model; it examines population-level accident occurrence using a
separate data design.

Empirical work has compared the share of accidents occurring in high wind with
the share of time that high wind was observed, an approach closely related to
the observed/expected comparison used here \citep{edwards1994}. Studies linking
crashes to nearby weather stations likewise show that station observations can
be informative while retaining measurement error \citep{young2007}. More broadly,
\citet{theofilatos2014} report less consistent road-safety evidence for wind
than for precipitation.

\section{Temperature, daylight, and time of day}

Temperature, daylight, and time of day add context that wind alone cannot
provide. They affect road-surface conditions, visibility, travel patterns, and
the composition of road users, but accident counts in these categories are not
rates unless the corresponding amount of travel is known. Temperature effects
can also be non-linear and season-specific: a Seoul time-series study found its
strongest cold-weather association below freezing in winter, while an hourly
study in Shenzhen found a J-shaped temperature relationship
\citep{lee2014,zhan2020}. Temperature is therefore compared with its local
seasonal frequency rather than treated as having one linear effect.

Darkness presents the same denominator problem. A method developed using
Norwegian, Swedish, and Dutch data compared daylight and darkness within clock
time and season, avoiding the interpretation of raw night-time counts as risk
\citep{johansson2009}. Here, raw hour, season, and daylight counts describe the
sample. Without hourly or daylight-specific traffic exposure, these counts cannot identify differences in accident risk.

\section{Traffic and accident rates}

Weather can change the amount of traffic as well as the hazard. Research using
automatic traffic recorders shows that extreme weather variables, including
wind, can reduce or redistribute hourly traffic volume
\citep{sathiaraj2018}. Accident counts during strong wind can remain low even if
risk to each travelling vehicle is higher. High-resolution crash studies
illustrate the value of hourly weather data rather than daily or monthly
averages \citep{xing2019}. The analyses distinguish
three quantities: accident occurrence relative to weather frequency,
rates based on estimated vehicle-kilometres, and changes in observed daily
traffic.

These studies show why weather frequency and traffic exposure need to be
considered separately when interpreting accident counts. The analyses therefore
begin with local weather-frequency standardisation and then introduce traffic
through an approximate correction and a VKT analysis.

\subsection{Accident data}

The accident dataset contains 126,607 records from 2007--2025
\citep{samgongustofaAccidents}. These events come from the official register, which is based primarily on police reports \citep{samgongustofaRegister}. One record lacks valid temporal or spatial
information, leaving 126,606 valid records.

Rural status is derived from accident coordinates and Statistics Iceland
urban-locality polygons \citep{hagstofaThettbyli}. Points inside an urban polygon
are classified as Urban, and valid points outside all polygons as Rural. A
defined Vestmannaeyjar area is also classified as Urban. The same 2020--2024
polygon dataset is applied to the full 2007--2025 accident period. Changes in
urban boundaries before 2020 are not represented.

Applying this rural classification yields 24,005 accidents, of which 6,414
involve injury across the rural road network (Figure~\ref{fig:accident-map}). Of these, 6,259 have a qualifying primary weather match
within 20 km and five minutes.

The selection follows the scientific focus: rural roads may be more directly exposed to local weather conditions, and rural records contain a larger injury share. Of 102,601 urban accidents, 10,631 involved injury (10.4\%), compared with 6,414 of 24,005 rural accidents (26.7\%). Among injury accidents, serious or fatal outcomes accounted for 1,802 urban records (17.0\%) and 1,471 rural records (22.9\%). These are recorded counts and proportions, not exposure-adjusted accident rates. The analysis first restricts to rural injury accidents, separates minor from serious or fatal injury, and then requires qualifying nearby weather. The maximum distance of 20 km is a pragmatic coverage/proximity compromise, not a physical safety threshold.

Accident severity is recorded in \texttt{meidsli}: code 1 denotes a fatal
accident, code 2 a serious accident, code 3 a minor-injury accident, and code 4
an accident without injury. The main outcome is
\(\texttt{meidsli}\leq3\). The serious or fatal comparison uses
\(\texttt{meidsli}\leq2\). The source data also contain 17,591 rural
damage-only accidents; these are outside the injury outcome.

For the reader-facing results, injury accidents are divided into two disjoint
groups: minor injury (\texttt{meidsli}=3) and serious or fatal injury
(\texttt{meidsli}=1 or 2). The O/E calculation for these groups is described
in Section~\ref{sec:weather-frequency}.

accident record before weather matching.

\subsection{Weather data}

The weather dataset contains 10-minute observations from Icelandic weather
stations. The variables used here are 10-minute mean wind speed (\texttt{f}), reported
wind gust (\texttt{fg}), and temperature (\texttt{t}). For each accident, the
gust is taken from the same matched 10-minute observation as mean wind; it is
not the maximum across the accident day. Mean wind speed remains the main
weather measure. A wind observation is used when

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:wind-validity}
0 \leq f < 45, \qquad 0 \leq fg < 65, \qquad fg + 0.5 \geq f,
\end{equation}

where \(f\) and \(fg\) are mean wind speed and gust, respectively, in m/s.

Rows missing either \texttt{f} or \texttt{fg}, values outside these bounds,
and internally inconsistent records where \texttt{fg = 0} but \texttt{f > 0}
are excluded. A value of \texttt{f = 0} alone is retained; only uninterrupted
runs with \texttt{f = fg = 0} lasting at least two hours are excluded as
potential sensor stoppages. Table~\ref{tab:weather-cleaning} gives the full
cleaning summary. Missing temperature does not affect wind eligibility.

Each accident is matched to the geographically nearest eligible wind station
within 20 km, using the closer of the two adjacent 10-minute timestamps. The
selected observation is therefore within five minutes of the recorded accident
time and can fall on the neighbouring calendar day for accidents close to
midnight. Matching eligibility is recorded for all 6,414 rural injury accidents.

Temperature is matched independently within the same 20 km and five-minute
limits. The nearest valid value between $-30$ and
$30\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ may therefore come from a different station when the
wind station lacks temperature. In the final analysis dataset, however, all
6,259 wind and temperature matches use the same station and observation time.

The same cleaned weather archive is used for accident matching, background
frequencies and traffic analyses; temperature is not
imputed.

Table~\ref{tab:match-quality} shows the coverage gained as the matching radius
increases. A distant station is a less desirable proxy for road conditions,
but there is no physically exact cut-off. The 20 km threshold was selected as
a practical compromise between high coverage and avoiding very distant stations;
it was not determined by an optimisation procedure.

\subsection{Traffic data}

Daily counters provide the traffic information used in the main analysis.
Seasonal road-section averages are also available for 2000--2025
\citep{vegagerdinTraffic}, and provide the coarser full-period exposure approximation used for the descriptive winter--summer and road-rate summaries. They do not replace daily counts in the main VKT weather analysis.

The study seasons follow the traffic periods: winter is December--March,
spring April--May, summer June--September, and autumn October--November. These
are operational, traffic-aligned seasons rather than conventional
meteorological seasons; the IMO also uses June--September when summarising the
Icelandic summer \citep{vedurSummer2025}.
Daily traffic counts from Vegagerðin cover 2019--2024 and underpin both the
traffic response and counter-linked exposure calculations. They are daily, not
hourly, totals. Counter positions are estimated from registered road geometry,
and more than one counter can occur on a road section. These data remain
separate from the primary O/E calculation.

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{Weather-frequency O/E analysis}
\label{sec:weather-frequency}

The observed-to-expected ratio (O/E) compares accidents observed in each
weather interval with the number expected from local weather frequency.
Accident-time mean wind (\texttt{f}) is the primary measure and is grouped in
5 m/s intervals; gust from the same 10-minute observation and independently
matched temperature are analysed in the same framework. For each measure,
expected counts use the weather distribution at the matched station in the same
study season, pooled across study years, rather than a national distribution.

The local background frequency is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:weather-frequency}
p_{jg}=\frac{T_{jg}}{T_g},
\end{equation}
where \(j\) indexes weather intervals, \(g\) identifies a station--season group,
\(T_{jg}\) counts its valid 10-minute observations in interval \(j\),
\(T_g\) counts all its valid observations, and \(p_{jg}\) is the resulting
weather-time proportion.

If accident timing followed local weather frequency, the expected number in an
interval would be the group accident total multiplied by its weather-time
proportion. The pooled observed-to-expected ratio is

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:weather-oe}
R_j=
\frac{\sum_g A_{jg}}
     {\sum_g A_g p_{jg}},
\end{equation}

where \(R_j\) is O/E for weather interval \(j\), \(A_{jg}\) is its observed
accident count in station--season group \(g\), \(A_g=\sum_j A_{jg}\) is the
group accident total, and \(p_{jg}\) is its background weather proportion.
The sums over \(g\) pool observed and expected counts across groups.

An O/E of one means that the accident share equals the local time share of the
weather interval; values above one indicate over-representation. For example,
56 minor-injury accidents occurred at mean wind of at least 20 m/s compared
with 25.18 expected, giving O/E 2.22. Standardising within station and season prevents differences in local weather
distributions from dominating the pooled ratio.

Minor injury (\textit{meiðsli}\(=3\)) and serious or fatal injury
(\textit{meiðsli}\(\leq2\)) are analysed as disjoint groups, each with its
own station--season expected counts. Combined all-injury O/E uses summed
observed and expected counts rather than averaging group ratios. O/E estimates are descriptive. Confidence intervals were not calculated,
and differences between categories were not tested for statistical significance.

Temperature was grouped into 3$^{\circ}$C intervals from $-6$ to 12$^{\circ}$C, with open-ended groups below $-6$$^{\circ}$C and at or above 12$^{\circ}$C.

The original 5 m/s intervals are the normal descriptive scale for wind. The pooled primary contrast uses the extreme mean-wind category of at least 20 m/s. The coarse joint summary and some seasonal displays combine mean winds from 15 m/s to avoid excessively sparse subdivisions; the detailed joint figure retains 15--20 and at least 20 m/s as separate groups. These grouping choices were not statistically optimised and are not universal physical danger thresholds.

The detailed joint wind--temperature figure uses mean-wind intervals $[0,5]$, $[5,10]$, $[10,15]$, $[15,20]$ and $\geq20$ m/s, and temperature intervals $<-6$, $[-6,-3]$, $[-3,0]$, $[0,3]$, $[3,6]$, $[6,9]$, $[9,12]$ and $\geq12$ $^{\circ}$C, within the existing weather-validity limits. These are descriptive categories, not physical safety thresholds. Finite intervals include the lower bound and exclude the upper bound. Square brackets are used in display labels for readability.

Computationally, each simultaneous valid wind and temperature observation enters exactly one of the 40 cells. Expected counts use the actual joint frequency within the same station--season groups as Equation~\ref{eq:weather-oe}, rather than products of marginal frequencies. Accident matches must share station and observation time. The 40 cells can be pooled into the coarser wind $<15$/\(\geq15\) m/s and temperature $<3$/\(\geq3\)$^{\circ}$C summary; observed and expected totals each equal the eligible joint sample.

The joint description uses weather-frequency O/E because simultaneous observations and the larger 2007--2025 matched sample support a joint weather-time denominator. The 694-accident VKT sample instead uses allocated station--month exposure, not directly observed simultaneous wind--temperature travel. Cell O/E therefore describes accident occurrence relative to local weather frequency, not a vehicle-based rate. Cells with fewer than 10 observed or 5 expected accidents are marked as sparse. For the supporting grouped contrasts, observed and expected counts are summed across combined intervals before O/E is calculated. Descriptive contrasts divide O/E at wind \(\geq15\) m/s by O/E at 0--5 m/s within temperature groups, and O/E at \(\geq12\)$^{\circ}$C or $-3$--0$^{\circ}$C by O/E at 6--12$^{\circ}$C within wind groups. Contrasts are reported only when both cells meet the support rule. They are not relative risks or formal interaction estimates.

\subsection{Use of traffic data}

The primary O/E denominator is the local frequency of wind conditions rather
than traffic volume. Complete traffic data are unavailable at the required
spatial and temporal resolution, so the weather-frequency O/E analysis does not account for changes in travel
volume during strong wind. If traffic is lower in strong wind while other
factors remain unchanged, the accident-rate contrast per vehicle-kilometre
would be larger than the weather-frequency O/E contrast.

Traffic information is used in two distinct ways. The approximate correction
estimates the relative daily traffic response across weather intervals from the
2019--2024 counters and uses that response to reweight the weather-frequency O/E
denominator. The VKT analysis uses daily traffic and rural road
length to estimate exposure in a smaller counter-linked sample; its allocation
across weather intervals is described in Section~\ref{sec:vkt-analysis}. Neither analysis observes hourly traffic.

\subsection{Approximate traffic-corrected O/E analysis}

The approximate correction uses the observed day-to-day traffic response at
counter sections during 2019--2024. Among positive-count days, the mean count
for the same counter section, year, month, and weekday defines expected daily
traffic. Each day's observed 24-hour count and expected count are allocated
to weather intervals in proportion to their valid 07:00--24:00 weather minutes
on that date. For interval \(j\), \(r_j\) is the sum of allocated observed
traffic divided by the sum of allocated expected traffic. Zero-count days are
excluded from this response calculation. The resulting \(r_j\) is pooled
across eligible counter-days and counter sections, and is applied as a common
interval-specific response when reweighting station--season weather frequencies.

\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\setlength{\parskip}{0.5\baselineskip}
Using the same station--season group \(g\) and weather interval \(j\) as in the weather-frequency O/E analysis,
the traffic-weighted weather frequency is

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:traffic-frequency}
p^{*}_{jg}=\frac{p_{jg}r_j}{\sum_h p_{hg}r_h},
\end{equation}

where \(p^{*}_{jg}\) is the reweighted proportion for interval \(j\) in
station--season group \(g\), \(p_{jg}\) is its original weather proportion,
and \(r_j\) is the allocated observed-to-expected traffic ratio. The index
\(h\) runs over all weather intervals to normalise the weights.
\end{minipage}

Expected counts are then recalculated as \(A_g p^{*}_{jg}\), where \(A_g\) is the group accident total. Accident bins use
weather at the recorded accident time, whereas \(r_j\) is based on a 24-hour
traffic total allocated across weather intervals according to the observed
weather between 07:00 and 24:00. Daily
counters do not observe hourly traffic. The 2019--2024 response is also
extrapolated to the 2007--2025 primary population. The result is therefore an approximate traffic-reweighted O/E adjustment rather than a directly observed traffic-adjusted O/E.

\subsection{Vehicle-kilometres travelled (VKT) analysis}
\label{sec:vkt-analysis}

The main traffic rate is restricted to 2019--2024, when daily counter data are
available. Eligible accidents must be rural injury accidents occurring between
07:00 and 24:00, have a counter section for the registered road and year, be
successfully located on that section, have usable accident-time wind and gust
within 20 km and five minutes, and have a traffic record on the accident date.
For this analysis, the 20 km weather distance is measured from the
counter-section location rather than from the accident coordinate. The same
five-minute time rule applies as in the weather-frequency O/E analysis and can select the adjacent observation
across midnight. The denominator is broader than the accident set: it uses
every eligible recorded counter-section day with a nonnegative traffic count
and rural road length, including days without an accident. Estimated exposure, in vehicle-kilometres travelled (VKT), is

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:monthly-vkt}
\widehat V_{cdb}=C_{cd}L_{c,y(d)}p_{s(c),m(d),b},
\end{equation}

where \(\widehat V_{cdb}\) is exposure for counter section \(c\), date \(d\),
and weather interval \(b\); \(C_{cd}\) is the observed 24-hour traffic count;
\(L_{c,y(d)}\) is rural section length in kilometres for calendar year \(y(d)\);
\(m(d)\) is the calendar month; and \(s(c)\) is the weather station assigned to
section \(c\). The proportion \(p_{s(c),m(d),b}\) is the 07:00--24:00 weather
frequency for that station, month and interval. The weather frequencies are pooled across 2007--2025 to provide stable
station--month proportions, while both traffic and accident eligibility remain
restricted to 2019--2024. The full daily count is allocated among weather intervals using the
07:00--24:00 station--month weather proportions. These proportions sum to one
and therefore conserve total daily exposure. Accidents are
classified separately by observed event-time weather within 20 km and five
minutes.

Because accident eligibility and weather frequencies cover 07:00--24:00 but traffic counts cover 24 hours, absolute rates use allocated daily VKT rather than directly observed daytime VKT. Interpretation therefore emphasises contrasts between weather intervals rather than the absolute rate level.

\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\setlength{\parskip}{0.5\baselineskip}
The reported rate is

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:monthly-rate}
R_b=\frac{O_b}{\sum_c\sum_d\widehat V_{cdb}}10^6,
\end{equation}

where \(R_b\) is the accident rate per million estimated vehicle-km in weather interval
\(b\), \(O_b\) is its event-time accident count, and \(\widehat V_{cdb}\)
is allocated exposure summed over eligible counter sections \(c\) and dates
\(d\). The factor \(10^6\) expresses the rate per million estimated vehicle-km.
\end{minipage}

Observed daily traffic multiplied by rural road length determines each eligible
counter-day's total estimated VKT. Monthly weather frequency is used only to
allocate that VKT among weather intervals. The same allocation is applied separately to mean wind, gust and temperature using each variable's pooled station--month frequency; the proportions are normalised within each variable so that allocated exposure sums to the same total daily VKT. Because traffic is available only as
daily totals, the main analysis uses the pooled station--month weather
distribution rather than the weather realised on each individual day. This
preserves observed day-to-day traffic variation but does not reconstruct hourly
exposure. Actual event-time weather classifies the accident numerator.

For the injury-component figures, both rates share the exposure denominator:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:injury-rates}
R_b^{\mathrm{minor}}=\frac{O_b^{\mathrm{minor}}}{\widehat V_b}10^6,
\qquad
R_b^{\mathrm{serious/fatal}}=\frac{O_b^{\mathrm{serious/fatal}}}{\widehat V_b}10^6,
\end{equation}
where \(b\) indexes weather intervals, \(O_b^{\mathrm{minor}}\) and
\(O_b^{\mathrm{serious/fatal}}\) are the respective accident counts, and
\(\widehat V_b\) is total monthly-allocated exposure in that interval.
The corresponding \(R_b\) terms are rates per million estimated vehicle-km.
Minor injury means code 3; serious or fatal injury means codes 1--2. Their
counts are disjoint, so their rates sum to the all-injury rate. Blue forms
the bottom of each stack and red the top. This is a descriptive decomposition
of the accident numerator, not a severity model. For seasonal panels, counts
and exposure are summed within each study season before division; the seasons
partition the All year result. In the seasonal rate figures, mean-wind
intervals at or above 15 m/s and gust intervals at or above 20 m/s are combined
by summing counts and exposure before division. All year retains the original intervals.

\subsection{Comparison with traffic-corrected O/E}
\label{sec:method-comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:evidence-summary} summarises the primary mean-wind and gust results after rescaling each method to its own low-wind reference. The displayed ratios share an upper-to-reference interpretation, but the underlying samples and denominators still differ.

\input{generated/evidence.tex}


Temperature further illustrates why the methods should not be equated. Both approaches show a warm-end upturn, but VKT rates remain highest in cold conditions (see Figures~\ref{fig:temperature-oe-panels} and~\ref{fig:monthly-vkt-temperature}).

The estimates differ because they answer different questions. Weather-frequency O/E compares accident occurrence with local weather time. Its approximate correction reweights that time using the relative traffic response at selected counters, extrapolating 2019--2024 behaviour to the broader 2007--2025 accident population. VKT instead divides accidents by estimated travel in a smaller counter-linked 2019--2024 sample. The contributing roads, periods and exposure weights differ; identical ratio notation does not make the estimands identical.

The pooled VKT comparison also depends on where and when travel occurs. Windy and calm vehicle-kilometres need not come from the same mix of counter sections, years and seasons. If groups contributing more windy travel have higher accident rates overall, pooling can enlarge the wind ratio even without an equally large increase within each group. Counter placement on hazardous roads alone would not do this: those roads must contribute a different share of windy than of calm travel.

An exploratory diagnostic uses the same 694 VKT accidents but calculates expected counts within each counter-section--year--season group from its distribution of estimated VKT. The resulting upper-to-0--5 m/s ratios are 3.43 for mean wind and 5.16 for gust, compared with pooled VKT rate ratios of 5.83 and 7.78. Differences in exposure composition account arithmetically for factors of about 1.70 and 1.51 in the pooled ratios. This does not establish that particular roads are intrinsically dangerous, nor does it remove the wind association within groups.

Other differences remain. Restricting corrected O/E to 2019--2024 gives ratios of 2.84 for mean wind and 5.95 for gust; the change of years therefore explains little of the mean-wind gap but some of the gust gap. The VKT upper bins each contain only 11 accidents, making their ratios sensitive to sampling variation. Moreover, VKT allocates daily traffic across wind intervals using pooled station--month weather frequencies, not observed travel during the actual windy hours. A comparison using one common accident sample, with uncertainty estimates and more direct traffic exposure, is needed to determine the contribution of each mechanism.



\subsection{Descriptive full-period road-rate comparison}
\label{sec:road-rates}

This descriptive road-level calculation is separate from the main counter-linked VKT analysis above. The full-period ranking aggregates injury accidents and annual estimated rural VKT by registered road number. Annual exposure is annual average daily traffic times mapped rural length times calendar days, the year-round counterpart of the winter and summer daily-traffic construction. Only accidents linked to road-section years with positive estimated exposure enter the numerator. Of the 6,414 rural injury accidents, 5,498 link to such section-years and 916 are excluded from the ranking numerator. The full unfiltered CSV retains every road with exposure, including zero-accident roads; it does not assume that a road is intrinsically dangerous.

The road ordering could be biased if the 916 accidents without a link to road-section years with positive estimated exposure are concentrated on particular roads. Whole-road aggregation does not identify hazardous locations within roads. This comparison is not weather-specific and does not attribute differences between roads to wind.

The unfiltered top ten contain eight roads with only one accident. Publishing those as a stable top ten would be misleading. For display, Table~\ref{tab:road-rates} restricts the ranking to roads with at least 20 linked injury accidents. Under a simple Poisson count approximation this limits count-only relative standard error to about 22\%; it does not address exposure error and is not a significance threshold. The unfiltered ranking remains the audit source. Sparse counts, changing identifiers and incomplete historical geometry still limit interpretation.

rate therefore differs from the ranking by accident count.

\chapter{Discussion}

\section{Interpretation}

Across the analyses, the strongest contrasts occur in the upper wind intervals. The primary weather-frequency result shows that strong wind is over-represented at rural injury accident times. The approximate traffic correction shows that lower traffic during strong wind does not explain that over-representation (see Figures~\ref{fig:traffic-corrected-wind} and~\ref{fig:traffic-corrected-gust}), and the VKT analysis retains the upper-wind increase when estimated travel exposure is introduced. The analyses therefore agree on the direction of the wind association, but they do not estimate the same quantity: their denominators differ and the VKT analysis uses a smaller counter-linked sample.

The correction remains approximate: the 2019--2024 counter response is extrapolated to the 2007--2025 population, and hourly traffic is unavailable.

The VKT analysis also shows higher rates in the upper wind
intervals, but its rate ratios are larger than the O/E contrasts
(see Table~\ref{tab:evidence-summary}). Those
magnitudes are uncertain: only 11 accidents fall in each highest wind or gust
interval, counter coverage is selective, and pooled rates
depend partly on which road sections contribute exposure. The smaller
within-counter-section--year--season contrasts suggest that road and exposure
composition explain part of the difference between pooled VKT rate ratios and
locally standardised O/E, without implying that particular roads are
intrinsically more dangerous.

Pooled station--month allocation conserves daily VKT but approximates the weather distribution of travel. Gust shows a similar upper-tail pattern, although mean wind and gust come from the same observations. The temperature association is non-linear: the O/E analyses show an excess just below freezing and at the warm end, whereas VKT rates are highest in cold intervals and rise again above the 9--12$^{\circ}$C minimum. The coldest O/E interval is below one, and the warm-end VKT rate remains well below the cold rates. The three analyses therefore do not support identical temperature contrasts (see Figures~\ref{fig:temperature-oe-panels}, \ref{fig:traffic-corrected-temperature} and~\ref{fig:monthly-vkt-temperature}).

\input{generated/joint_detail_discussion.tex}

Several alternative mechanisms could contribute to the joint pattern. Drivers may reduce speed, avoid travel or change routes when hazards are apparent, while warnings and closures may change who remains on the road. These are hypotheses, not evidence that drivers compensate when wind and near-freezing temperatures coincide. Drivers may perceive warm conditions as less hazardous and choose higher speeds. However, speed and perceived risk were not measured. Tourism, motorcycle use, recreational travel, vehicle mix and route choice could also contribute to the warm-weather excess. Correlated precipitation, ice, road-surface state and geography may also contribute. Behavioural adaptation, travel avoidance, closures, changing vehicle composition and direct physical effects of weather cannot be distinguished with the available data.

The full-period seasonal exposure summary similarly shows why raw counts can mislead: winter contained fewer injury accidents than summer, but the estimated injury-accident rate per rural VKT was about 1.92 times the summer rate because estimated winter travel was much lower.

The wind findings are consistent with earlier accident-frequency comparisons
\citep{edwards1994} and with engineering evidence that crosswind can impair
vehicle stability \citep{baker1986,baker1994risk}. The study links a long Icelandic accident series to local weather frequency
and examines the wind association using several traffic-exposure
constructions. The non-monotonic
temperature results are also compatible with
previous evidence of range- and season-dependent temperature associations
\citep{lee2014,zhan2020}, while the observed traffic reduction in strong wind
agrees with recorder-based evidence that adverse weather changes travel demand
\citep{sathiaraj2018}.

\section{Strengths}

The primary analysis combines a long accident series with 10-minute weather observations screened using explicit validity rules and standardises expected counts within station
and study season rather than against one national weather distribution. High
match coverage and explicit reporting of weather exclusions make the O/E
calculation transparent.

The two traffic analyses address the main limitation of the weather-frequency
denominator in different ways. The approximate correction reweights expected
counts using the observed counter response, whereas the VKT
analysis uses observed daily traffic, rural road length, and accident-free
counter-days to estimate exposure. The scripted workflow generates thesis tables and validates sample counts, matching rules and exposure conservation.

\section{Limitations}

The accident register does not capture every traffic injury. Because it is based primarily on police-reported accidents, minor injuries are particularly susceptible to underreporting; health institutions and insurers record additional cases that do not enter the official register \citep{samgongustofaRegister}.

Traffic exposure is the main limitation. The primary O/E uses weather time rather
than vehicle-kilometres, and the approximate correction extrapolates a
2019--2024 response from selected counters to the broader 2007--2025 population.
Because traffic is observed only as daily totals, the traffic analyses cannot
reconstruct the hourly exposure corresponding to accident-time weather.

The VKT analysis is also selective: it is limited to rural counter sections with
usable traffic, road geometry, and weather in 2019--2024. Its monthly weather
frequencies are pooled over 2007--2025 for stability, while traffic and accidents
remain restricted to the counter era. Only a few accidents occur in the highest wind and gust intervals, so the large rate ratios should not be treated as precise estimates of magnitude. A counter represents exposure on a road section and does not
show that an accident-involved vehicle passed that counter.

Weather stations are proxies for road conditions. A station may be up to 20 km
from an accident or counter location, and Icelandic terrain can produce large
variation over shorter distances. The fixed 2020--2024 urban-boundary dataset
is applied to the full accident period, so historical settlement-boundary
changes are not represented. Unusual traffic, road-surface conditions, visibility and closures are not controlled. More generally, unmeasured road characteristics, vehicle
type, speed, driver behaviour, and weather-related road management may
contribute to the observed patterns. The study therefore supports associations
and exposure contrasts, not causal effects of wind.

\section{Future work}

Future research should first improve the measurement of travel exposure. Hourly traffic counts would align traffic with accident-time weather and distinguish daytime travel from the 24-hour totals currently allocated to accidents eligible during 07:00--24:00. Broader counter coverage, verified coordinates and stable road identifiers would improve linkage and reduce dependence on a selective sample. Counter-placement audits should compare monitored and unmonitored roads, while independent estimates from traffic surveys or other travel records could validate the VKT denominator. These steps would help establish whether weather contrasts persist under more directly observed exposure.

Weather measurements should also better represent conditions along the road. Roadside sensors or evaluated higher-resolution meteorological models could reduce dependence on a station some distance from an accident. Wind direction relative to road orientation would distinguish crosswind, headwind and tailwind, while gust duration and short-term variability could reveal information lost in a single observation. Precipitation, visibility, road-surface temperature, snow and ice should be linked to maintenance records, warnings and closures. Validation against roadside observations would be needed before treating modelled weather as an improvement over the current measurements.

Behaviour and exposure response require separate observation. Speed measurements and repeated traffic records could test whether drivers slow down, avoid travel, change routes or respond to warnings as conditions deteriorate. Combining these records with closure times would help distinguish voluntary travel avoidance from enforced restrictions. With sufficient observations, later work could examine whether responses differ when several hazards occur together. The present analysis cannot distinguish behavioural adaptation from reduced exposure, and a lower accident count under combined hazards would not by itself demonstrate safer driving. Comparisons should therefore measure both travel volume and behaviour.

A complementary extension would examine whether the upper-wind association is concentrated in single-vehicle crashes, road departures, overturning or loss-of-control events, after auditing accident-type coding. This would connect the population-level association more directly to the crosswind mechanisms discussed in the Background. Separate person- and vehicle-level extracts supplied for 2007--2024 may support subgroup analyses, subject to completeness and coding audits. Those extracts alone exclude 2025. Including that year would require harmonising the separate 2025 deliveries already available. Vehicle-class codes may support comparisons among passenger cars, vans, heavy goods vehicles, buses and motorcycles. Without traffic exposure by vehicle class, these would describe accident composition under different weather conditions, not vehicle-class-specific accident risk. Road curvature, gradient, elevation and orientation could distinguish exposed coastal roads, mountain passes and sheltered routes. Multilevel or spatial models could separate within-road weather variation from between-road differences, with reliable identifiers and exposure and allowance for dependence within roads and weather episodes.

Table~\ref{tab:road-rates} provides a starting point for closer examination of individual
roads. Future work could compare accident types, severity, season and
weather on these roads with patterns on roads with similar traffic and
geometry. Examining shorter road sections could show whether accidents
are concentrated at particular locations. More accurate traffic estimates
and data from later years would help assess whether the high estimated
rates persist.

Statistical development should quantify uncertainty in O/E and VKT contrasts and assess sensitivity to weather bins, matching distance, temporal tolerance, season definitions and exposure-allocation assumptions. Resampling or other uncertainty methods should preserve relevant station, road and temporal dependence. Flexible non-linear models could examine temperature without relying solely on grouped intervals. Formal wind--temperature interaction should be considered only with adequate joint sample size and support across the exposure range. Validation in later years would test whether estimated patterns generalise beyond the study period, while comparison with simpler models would assess whether added complexity improves interpretation or predictive performance.

\begin{samepage}
Around freezing, measured temperature should be compared with police-recorded road-surface or road-condition fields, if obtained and sufficiently complete, to assess whether the elevation immediately below freezing coincides with recorded ice or snow. At the warm end, the available person and vehicle data may help examine motorcycle involvement, injured road-user age or road-user type. They contain no confirmed tourism indicator. Such comparisons would assess composition rather than assume that ambient temperature changes driver care.\par
\end{samepage}

The accompanying \emph{Töflulýsingar í slysaskrá} describes the source database schema, not the completeness of delivered fields. Fields such as recorded cause, reported weather and actual speed according to the party may be subjective or incomplete and are not included in the delivered extracts. Future use should begin with missingness, coding-consistency and temporal-coverage audits. Following independent validation, findings might inform targeted warnings, monitoring, counter placement or road management. The present analysis is not ready for operational prediction, and more covariates or more complex models would not by themselves establish causation.

\chapter{Conclusion}

Strong wind was the clearest and most consistent weather association in the study. Rural injury accidents were substantially over-represented during strong wind relative to how often those conditions occurred locally: at mean wind speeds of at least 20 m/s, O/E was 2.15. In the second step, observed lower daily traffic in strong wind did not explain away the pattern; the approximate correction strengthened the upper-wind contrast. The third step, the smaller counter-linked VKT analysis, also showed higher rates in the strongest wind conditions, although their magnitude remains uncertain because counter coverage is selective and only 11 accidents occur in each highest wind or gust category.

Temperature showed a non-linear pattern. Both O/E analyses found elevated occurrence just below freezing and at temperatures of at least 12$^{\circ}$C, with lower relative occurrence at moderate temperatures. The coldest interval was not elevated above local weather frequency. VKT rates were highest in cold conditions and rose again at the warm end relative to the 9--12$^{\circ}$C minimum. The retained analyses support non-linearity, not one common temperature effect. The warm-temperature increase should be treated as an association requiring further study rather than evidence of a direct causal effect.

These findings describe associations, not causation or a direct national risk estimate. Exposure data remain the main limitation. Verified hourly traffic, broader counter coverage, stable locations and identifiers, and better measurement of road-level exposure composition would improve the comparison. Future work should incorporate vehicle class, wind direction relative to the road, road geometry, road-surface and closure information, and investigate the unexpected warm-temperature pattern.

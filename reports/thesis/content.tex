\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Context and Motivation}

Weather affects both driving conditions and the decision to travel. Strong
wind can make a vehicle harder to control, low temperature can coincide with
slippery roads, and darkness can reduce visibility. These conditions are
relevant to Icelandic roads, where terrain can produce substantial local
variation in wind \citep{snaebjornsson2007,pu2018}. Precipitation and fog are
also relevant road-weather hazards, but they are context for this study and
are not analysed here.

Previous research provides two reasons to examine wind. Engineering studies
describe mechanisms by which crosswind can destabilise vehicles, while
observational studies compare accident occurrence with the frequency of windy
conditions \citep{baker1986,baker1994risk,edwards1994}. These are complementary
forms of evidence: a physical mechanism does not establish how often accidents
occur across a road network. Reviews also find less consistent road-safety
evidence for wind than for precipitation \citep{theofilatos2014}. Local
observations are therefore needed to assess the pattern in the setting under
study.

In Iceland, wind-related road safety has been studied through vehicle
reliability and operational vehicle-wind models
\citep{snaebjornsson2007,hrafnkelsdottir2020}. The question addressed here is
different: how does recorded rural injury-accident occurrence vary with the
weather that is observed locally? The contribution is to connect a long
Icelandic accident series with station- and season-specific weather frequency,
then assess the interpretation through matched-time and traffic comparisons.
This links the engineering motivation to population-level accident records
without treating station wind as a measurement of the force on a vehicle.

This thesis studies rural accidents involving injury in Iceland from
2007--2025. Accident times and locations are linked to nearby 10-minute
weather observations. The main comparison concerns mean wind speed. Wind
gusts, temperature, season, time of day, daylight, and the available traffic
data are then examined separately.

Accident counts alone do not measure accident risk. Severe weather can increase the
hazard faced by each vehicle while reducing the number of vehicles on the
road. Weather frequency and traffic volume are therefore considered
separately \citep{andrey2003,lord2010,washington2020}.

\section{Aim and Research Questions}

The aim is to quantify how the occurrence of rural injury accidents varies
with weather and what the available traffic data add to the mean-wind result.
Three questions guide the analysis:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Q1 --- Primary weather-frequency O/E:} How does accident
  occurrence relative to local weather frequency vary across mean-wind,
  wind-gust and temperature intervals, overall and by season? The figures
  distinguish minor injury from serious or fatal injury; the combined
  all-injury result provides the main comparison across methods.
  \item \textbf{Q2 --- Matched-time robustness comparison:} Is the
  weather-frequency pattern supported when each accident time is compared
  with matched non-accident times at the same station and recurring calendar time?
  \item \textbf{Q3 --- Supporting traffic/exposure analyses:} What do the
  available annual and daily traffic data add to the interpretation of the
  wind pattern, given their coverage and exposure-allocation assumptions?
\end{enumerate}

The primary 2007--2025 analysis addresses the first question, the matched-time
analysis the second, and the smaller traffic-linked samples the third.
Minor-injury and serious-or-fatal accidents together form the all-injury
outcome. The study
estimates associations, not causal effects or a complete national accident
rate per vehicle-kilometre.

\section{Thesis Outline}

Chapter 2 gives the background for the weather and traffic comparisons.
Chapter 3 describes the data, data preparation, and statistical methods.
Chapter 4 presents the results, beginning with mean wind. Chapter 5 discusses
their interpretation and limitations, and Chapter 6 gives the conclusions.
The appendix contains selected checks that support interpretation of the main

\chapter{Background}

\section{Wind and Rural Road Accidents}

Crosswind can produce overturning, lateral sideslip, or excessive yaw. The
critical condition depends on vehicle geometry and mass, vehicle speed, road
friction, wind direction, and driver response rather than wind speed alone
\citep{baker1986,baker1994risk}. High-sided vehicles are particularly exposed,
but the physical mechanisms also provide a reason to examine loss-of-control
accidents more generally. These engineering studies establish physical
plausibility and vehicle-specific failure mechanisms; they do not by
themselves establish a population-level association between wind and accident
occurrence.

This problem is directly relevant in Iceland, where terrain can accelerate and
redirect near-surface wind over short distances. An Icelandic reliability
study by \citet{snaebjornsson2007} modelled accident probability as the combined
outcome of wind velocity and direction, road camber and friction, and vehicle speed.
A station measures wind at one point, whereas vehicle stability also depends
on the wind's direction relative to the road and on local road conditions.
Terrain between a station and a road can alter the wind reaching the vehicle.
Station observations therefore remain exposure proxies, and a shorter
matching distance does not guarantee identical conditions. Mobile road measurement
research similarly shows that roadside terrain creates marked
spatial variation that a sparse fixed-station network cannot fully resolve
\citep{pu2018}.

Vegager\dh in has developed a
vehicle-wind model to support decisions about hazardous conditions and road
closures, while also noting the limited experimental evidence available for
passenger cars \citep{hrafnkelsdottir2020}. The present thesis does not test that
operational model; it examines population-level accident occurrence using a
separate data design.

Empirical work has compared the share of accidents occurring in high wind with
the share of time that high wind was observed, an approach closely related to
the observed/expected comparison used here \citep{edwards1994}. Studies linking
crashes to nearby weather stations likewise show that station observations can
be informative while retaining measurement error \citep{young2007}. More broadly,
\citet{theofilatos2014} report less consistent road-safety evidence for wind
than for precipitation.

\section{Temperature, Daylight, and Time of Day}

Temperature, daylight, and time of day add context that wind alone cannot
provide. They affect road-surface conditions, visibility, travel patterns, and
the composition of road users, but accident counts in these categories are not
rates unless the corresponding amount of travel is known. Temperature effects
can also be non-linear and season-specific: a Seoul time-series study found its
strongest cold-weather association below freezing in winter, while an hourly
case-crossover study in Shenzhen found a J-shaped temperature relationship
\citep{lee2014,zhan2020}. Temperature is therefore compared with its local
seasonal frequency rather than treated as having one linear effect.

Darkness presents the same denominator problem. A method developed using
Norwegian, Swedish, and Dutch data compared daylight and darkness within clock
time and season, avoiding the interpretation of raw night-time counts as risk
\citep{johansson2009}. Here, raw hour, season, and daylight counts describe the
sample. Matched daylight and adjusted severity models are used for numerical
comparisons.

\section{Traffic and Accident Rates}

Weather can change the amount of traffic as well as the hazard. Research using
automatic traffic recorders shows that extreme weather variables, including
wind, can reduce or redistribute hourly traffic volume
\citep{sathiaraj2018}. Accident counts during strong wind can remain low even if
risk to each travelling vehicle increases. High-resolution crash studies show
the value of hourly weather and matched-time analyses rather than daily or
monthly averages \citep{xing2019}. The analyses distinguish three
quantities: accident occurrence relative to weather frequency,
rates based on estimated vehicle-kilometres, and changes in observed daily
traffic. Studies with real-time traffic, weather, and road geometry show what
a more complete analysis could achieve when such data are available
\citep{ahmed2012}.

\subsection{Accident Data}

The accident dataset contains 126,607 records from 2007--2025
\citep{samgongustofaAccidents}. One record lacks valid temporal or spatial
information, leaving 126,606 valid records.

Rural status is derived from accident coordinates and Statistics Iceland
urban-locality polygons \citep{hagstofaThettbyli}. Points inside an urban polygon
are classified as Urban, and valid points outside all polygons as Rural. A
defined Vestmannaeyjar area is also classified as Urban. The same 2020--2024
polygon dataset is applied to the full 2007--2025 accident period. Changes in
urban boundaries before 2020 are not represented.

Applying this rural classification yields 24,005 accidents, of which 6,414
involve injury. Of these, 6,259 have a qualifying primary weather match
within 20 km and five minutes.

Accident severity is recorded in \texttt{meidsli}: code 1 denotes a fatal
accident, code 2 a serious accident, code 3 a minor-injury accident, and code 4
an accident without injury. The main outcome is
\(\texttt{meidsli}\leq3\). The serious-or-fatal comparison uses
\(\texttt{meidsli}\leq2\). The source data also contain 17,591 rural
damage-only accidents; these are outside the injury outcome.

The O/E figures divide the injury outcome into two separate groups: blue
for minor injury (\texttt{meidsli}=3), and red for serious or fatal injury
(\texttt{meidsli}=1 or 2). An accident appears in only one group.
Table~\ref{tab:windy-group-examples} gives a worked example of each group
in windy conditions. Expected counts are calculated separately for each
group using its station--season accident totals and local weather frequencies
(the full formula is given below). For minor injury, 56 observed accidents
divided by 25.18 expected gives O/E 2.22; for serious or fatal injury,
12 divided by 6.41 gives O/E 1.87. These pooled examples illustrate the
calculation, not the cause of any individual accident.

\subsection{Weather Data}

The weather dataset contains 10-minute observations from Icelandic weather
stations. The variables used here are 10-minute mean wind speed (\texttt{f}), reported
wind gust (\texttt{fg}), and temperature (\texttt{t}). For each accident, the
gust is taken from the same matched 10-minute observation as mean wind; it is
not the maximum across the accident day. Mean wind speed remains the main
weather measure. A wind observation is used when

\[
0 \leq f < 45, \qquad 0 \leq fg < 65, \qquad fg + 0.5 \geq f.
\]

Rows missing either \texttt{f} or \texttt{fg} are excluded. Negative values,
values at or above either upper limit, and
internally inconsistent values where \texttt{fg = 0} but \texttt{f > 0} are
excluded and reported in the annual audit. A value of \texttt{f = 0} alone is
kept. Only uninterrupted all-zero runs where \texttt{f = fg = 0} for at
least two hours are excluded as potentially frozen sensors. Missing temperature
does not affect whether a wind observation is used.

Each accident is matched to the geographically nearest station with a
quality-controlled
wind observation at one of the two adjacent timestamps on the 10-minute observation
grid. The observations have 10-minute resolution, but the selected
observation is no more than five minutes from the recorded accident time. The
maximum matching distance is 20 km. The matching dataset includes all 6,414
accidents and records whether each accident meets the weather-match criterion.

Temperature is matched independently within the same 20 km and five-minute
limits. The nearest valid value between $-30$ and
$30\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ may therefore come from a different station when the
wind station lacks temperature. In the final analysis dataset, however, all
6,259 wind and temperature matches use the same station and observation time.

The IMO supplied observations of time, station, temperature, mean wind, and
gust. The same quality-controlled weather data are used for accident matching,
background frequencies, matched control times, and traffic analyses. No
temperature value is imputed.

Throughout the analyses, the four study seasons are aligned with these
traffic periods: winter is December--March, spring is April--May, summer is
June--September, and autumn is October--November. These are study-specific
traffic-aligned seasons rather than conventional three-month meteorological
seasons. Using June--September as summer keeps the weather comparison aligned
with the Icelandic road authority's summer traffic period; it is an operational
definition, not a claim that weather is uniform across those months. The IMO
also uses June--September when summarising the Icelandic summer
\citep{vedurSummer2025}.
VHDU is not calculated as \(ADU-SDU-VDU\). Annual traffic implied by
ADU is first converted to a calendar-year total; the day-weighted SDU and VDU
totals are subtracted, and the remainder is divided by the number of spring and
autumn days:
\[
VHDU=\frac{D_{year}ADU-D_{VDU}VDU-D_{SDU}SDU}{D_{VHDU}}.
\]
Here, each \(D\) is the number of calendar days in that period. VHDU is
therefore a residual estimate rather than an additional official traffic
measure.

Daily traffic counts extracted from Vegagerðin reports cover 2019--2024 and
provide observed totals at selected counter sites. They support both the
traffic-response comparison and secondary daily-counter accident-rate
analyses, but do not provide hourly vehicle-kilometres at every accident
location. More than one counter may occur on a road section, and counter
positions are estimated from the registered road geometry. These data remain
separate from the primary O/E calculation.

Of 68,946 annual road-section--year--period records, 61,442 have nearby usable
wind data; separately, 763,171 of 774,274 daily counter-days have a matched
daytime wind summary. The smaller linked accident samples used by the
traffic models are reported in Chapter 4.

\section{Analysis}

\subsection{Weather-Frequency Comparison}

Accident-time mean wind speed \texttt{f} is the main weather
measure and is divided into 5 m/s intervals. It is the 10-minute mean wind
observation matched within five minutes of the accident time, not an
instantaneous measurement. Wind gust \texttt{fg} from the same observation and
independently matched temperature are additional weather measures. For each
measure, an accident is compared with the distribution at its matched station
in the same study season, pooled across all study years. Expected
counts are based on how often each condition occurred locally rather
than on a national weather distribution.
For example, if 5\% of the relevant station--season observations fall in one
wind interval, approximately 5\% of that group's accidents would be expected
there under weather-frequency-proportional occurrence.

Let \(g\) identify a weather station and study season.
Let \(A_{jg}\) be the number of accidents in weather interval \(j\) and group \(g\),
and let \(A_g=\sum_j A_{jg}\). From all quality-controlled 10-minute observations, let
\(T_{jg}\) be the number in interval \(j\) and \(T_g\) the total in group \(g\).
The local background frequency is

\[
p_{jg}=\frac{T_{jg}}{T_g}.
\]

If accident timing followed local wind frequency, the expected number in the
interval would be \(A_g p_{jg}\). The pooled observed/expected ratio is

\[
R_j=
\frac{\sum_g A_{jg}}
     {\sum_g A_g p_{jg}}.
\]

A value of one means that the accident share equals the local time share of the
weather interval. A value above one means that accidents are over-represented
in that interval. Standardisation within station and season prevents locations
or seasons with different weather distributions from dominating the result.

The figures show the calculation separately for minor-injury accidents
(\textit{meiðsli}\(=3\)) and serious-or-fatal accidents
(\textit{meiðsli}\(\leq2\)). These groups do not overlap. Each group's
expected count uses its own accident totals within station and season.
The combined all-injury O/E is also retained for comparison with the other
analyses; it is calculated from summed observed and expected counts, not
by averaging the two group ratios. The O/E values are
descriptive and are therefore shown without confidence intervals. Wind gust
and temperature are handled in the same way as mean wind. Temperature is
matched independently, and its intervals are below \(-6\), \(-6\) to \(-3\),
\(-3\) to 0, 0 to 3, 3 to 6, 6 to 9, 9 to 12, and at least
12\(^{\circ}\)C.
The O/E upper interval is combined at 12\(^{\circ}\)C to keep the seasonal
panels readable. The matched-time and annual-traffic models retain separate
12--15 and at-least-15\(^{\circ}\)C intervals. Estimates across those different
upper intervals are not treated as direct numerical comparisons.

\subsection{Matched-Time Comparison}

This is a time-stratified case-crossover design: each accident forms one
matched set. The accident time is
the case time, and the control times are the other occurrences of the same
weekday and clock time in the same calendar month and year. For example, a
Tuesday accident at 14:20 is compared with the other Tuesdays at 14:20 in that
month. Mean-wind and gust controls use the accident's matched wind station; temperature
controls use its independently matched temperature station. Control weather
must satisfy the same five-minute observation-time rule, and both station
matches are restricted to 20 km. Conditional logistic regression compares
weather at the accident time with weather at its matched control times. This
time-stratified referent strategy was selected to avoid overlap bias and to
control calendar time without selecting controls from outside the accident
month \citep{janes2005}.

The model asks whether weather at an accident time differs from weather at
comparable non-accident times. Matching controls exactly for station, year,
month, weekday, and clock time, but not for unusual traffic, road conditions,
or other factors that change on a particular day. Continuous effects are
reported per 5 m/s or 5\(^{\circ}\)C together with categorical estimates. The
mean-wind O/E remains the primary result.

Mean wind and temperature are also entered together as categorical variables
to assess whether the wind estimate changes after including temperature at the
same case and control times. Daylight is
calculated at the accident location for the accident date and the same matched
dates. Because month, weekday, and clock time are held fixed, only strata that
change between darkness, civil twilight, and daylight contribute information.

Seasonal differences in the mean-wind association are tested in a separate
matched-time model. Mean wind is grouped as 0--10, 10--15, and at least
15 m/s for stability. The model includes interactions between wind interval and
season, and a likelihood-ratio test compares it with a model having one common
wind association across seasons. The omnibus test is used instead of judging
seasonal differences from separate confidence intervals.

A separate logistic model describes serious-or-fatal rather than minor injury
among accidents with complete wind and temperature matches. It includes broad
categories of mean wind, temperature, daylight, time of day, and season. The
result concerns injury severity among recorded accidents. It is not a model of
whether an accident occurs and does not replace the traffic analyses.

\subsection{Use of Traffic Data}

The primary O/E denominator is the local frequency of wind conditions rather
than traffic volume. Complete traffic data are unavailable at the required
spatial and temporal resolution. O/E therefore does not account for fewer
journeys on windy days. If traffic falls in strong wind while other factors
remain unchanged, the accident-rate contrast per vehicle-kilometre would be
larger than the O/E contrast. The traffic analyses examine this possibility.
The descriptive estimated-rate calculation uses annual traffic on every
eligible road section, including sections without an accident. The main
conditional model has a narrower purpose and sample: its analysis retains 5,125
accidents in road-section--year--traffic-period strata that contain an accident
and have a valid weather and traffic link. Both calculations use SDU and VDU
where they are published,
and the residual VHDU estimate for April--May and
October--November.

Adding these traffic data to the primary O/E calculation would replace the
broad study population with a smaller linked sample and combine seasonal
traffic averages with 10-minute weather. O/E is therefore interpreted as
relative accident occurrence, not accident rate per vehicle.

\subsection{Road-Section Traffic Comparison}

Traffic is included when accidents can be linked by road section, year,
official traffic period, and wind interval
\(k,j\), estimated vehicle-kilometres are

\[
V_{kj}=q_k L_k D_k p_{kj},
\]

where \(q_k\) is SDU, VDU, or derived VHDU vehicles per day, \(L_k\) is section length,
\(D_k\) is the number of calendar days, and \(p_{kj}\) is the local share of
quality-controlled 10-minute observations in the wind interval. The accident rate is

\[
\text{Rate}_j=
\frac{A_j}{\sum_k V_{kj}}\times 100{,}000{,}000.
\]

This method accounts for road length and for spatial and seasonal differences
in published traffic. It still assumes that traffic within a traffic period is
distributed across wind intervals in proportion to time. It does not observe
traffic at the accident minute. The station nearest the road-section midpoint
with qualifying data supplies \(p_{kj}\). Each accident is matched to that
road-section station rather than the accident-nearest station used in the
primary O/E analysis. An accident is included only when the
road-section station is within 20 km and has a valid observation within five
minutes. The accident count and its estimated denominator therefore use the
same weather station.

The main traffic result is a conditional Poisson model. Each road section,
year, and traffic period has its own intercept. For stratum \(k\) and wind
interval \(j\), the model is

\[
\log E(A_{kj}) = \alpha_k + \beta_j + \log V_{kj},
\]

where \(A_{kj}\) is the accident count and \(\log V_{kj}\) is the offset.
The exponentiated coefficient \(\exp(\beta_j)\) is the estimated rate ratio
relative to 0--5 m/s within otherwise identical road-section--year--period
strata.

The model is fitted separately for all injury accidents and for serious or
fatal accidents. A seasonal extension uses year-specific station frequencies
for winter, spring, summer, and autumn. Because individual 5 m/s upper bins are
sparse within seasons, that extension uses 0--10, 10--15, and at least 15 m/s.
Winter and summer use published VDU and SDU, respectively; spring and autumn
use the same derived VHDU daily volume but retain separate weather frequencies
and accident counts.

The descriptive rate and conditional model include different road records.
The descriptive rate sums estimated vehicle-kilometres over every eligible road section.
The conditional model includes only road-section--year--period strata containing
at least one accident, because all-zero strata provide no within-stratum
information: after conditioning on a stratum's total accident count, a total of
zero contributes no likelihood for comparing its wind intervals. Those roads
remain in the separate descriptive rate that sums all eligible exposure. Wind
intervals with positive estimated vehicle-kilometres are kept within those
groups. The two traffic results answer different questions and are not
expected to reproduce the same rate ratios.

\subsection{Daily Traffic Comparison}

Daily traffic is analysed separately using daily weather summaries.
Directional channels are first summed to one 24-hour count for each physical
counter and date. The unit is then one fixed counter on one date; separate
counter sites on the same road section are not summed. For each counter, year,
month, and weekday, expected traffic is the arithmetic mean of the available
daily counts in that same group. The traffic index is

\[
I_{cd}=100\times
\frac{Q_{cd}}
     {\operatorname{mean}(Q_{c,\,\text{same year, month, weekday}})}.
\]

An index below 100 indicates fewer counted vehicles than the mean for that
local calendar context. Results are aggregated across counter-days and describe
the participating sites rather than national traffic. The 24-hour count is not
converted into hourly traffic. Mean wind is calculated from quality-controlled observations
between 10:00 and 21:59, so this is a daily association rather than an estimate
of traffic in that window.
The daily-counter dataset contains 5,432 counter-days with a reported count of
zero. They are
kept because the reports do not distinguish a genuine zero or road closure
from counter downtime; the appendix reports a check excluding these records.
For each located counter, the nearest station within 20 km having usable
observations that day is selected.

Sustained wind is described by the number of valid 10-minute observations in
each counter-day with mean wind of at least 15 m/s; six observations equal one
hour. Days must contain at least 108 valid observations. Traffic is compared
across 0, more than 0--2, 2--6, and at least 6 hours of strong wind using the
same counter--calendar standardisation.

The allocated daily-counter rate model uses a stricter accident linkage. Each
2019--2024 accident requires a counter on the exact registered road section and
year within 20 km, positive traffic on its date, and qualifying accident-time
mean wind. For counter (c), date (d), and wind interval (j), the observed
24-hour count is allocated according to the counter station's valid 10-minute
observations:

\[
\widehat Q_{cdj}=Q_{cd}\frac{N_{cdj}}{N_{cd}}.
\]

Accidents are classified by accident-time \texttt{f} from the same station used
for the counter-day denominator. That station must be within 20 km of both the
accident and counter, and the accident observation must be within five minutes
of the recorded time. Estimated
traffic and accidents are aggregated by counter, year, and wind interval, and a
conditional Poisson model gives each counter--year its own intercept. The
allocation assumes uniform traffic across valid observation times; it is an
estimated within-day traffic split, not observed hourly traffic. The
main allocation uses all sufficiently complete 24-hour weather records.

A second daily-counter calculation uses the counter-section construction.
For each counter-section and day, the observed daily vehicle count is
multiplied by the rural part of the assigned road length. This daily
vehicle-kilometre total is allocated among weather intervals using valid
10-minute observations from the nearest available station within 20 km of the
counter location at each timestamp. The exposure window is 07:00--24:00;
the boundary observations at 07:00 and the following midnight each represent
five minutes. Accidents outside this time window are omitted. For day \(i\),
counter-section \(j\), and interval \(b\),

\[
\widehat V_{ijb}=Q_{ij}L^{\mathrm{rural}}_j\frac{M_{ijb}}{1020},
\]

where \(Q_{ij}\) is the observed 24-hour count,
\(L^{\mathrm{rural}}_j\) is the rural counter-section length, and \(M_{ijb}\)
is the number of represented minutes with valid same-day weather in interval
\(b\). Unobserved minutes contribute no exposure rather than being
renormalised to a complete day. Accident-time weather is taken from the same
time-specific station-selection rule within five minutes of the recorded
time. The accident rate is the observed accidents divided by summed
\(\widehat V_{ijb}\). This compares
accidents per estimated vehicle-kilometre, but the weather-bin allocation is estimated:
hourly traffic is not observed. Unlike the annual method, observed traffic on a
windy date contributes its own daily total, so lower travel on that date is
reflected in the denominator.

\chapter{Results}

Results follow the three research questions: weather-frequency O/E,
matched-time comparisons, and traffic analyses in smaller linked samples.
The main question is whether accidents are over-represented in strong wind
relative to its local frequency. The subsequent comparisons assess whether
that pattern persists under different time controls and exposure assumptions.
Agreement is assessed by the pattern and uncertainty, not by treating O/E,
odds ratios and estimated traffic-based rates as equivalent quantities.

when all seasons are pooled. The blue and red bars must nevertheless be read
as separate descriptive comparisons: their heights alone do not test whether

\subsection{Wind Gust}

Accident-time wind gust is an additional weather measure rather than a daily
maximum. Figure~\ref{fig:gust-oe-panels} shows that over-representation is
concentrated towards the upper gust intervals. At 30 m/s or above,
52 minor-injury accidents were observed compared
with 14.6 expected (O/E 3.56). The serious-or-fatal group contained nine
accidents compared with 3.6 expected (O/E 2.47). Gust and mean wind are related
measures and should not be treated as independent findings. Across both groups,
the all-injury result remains 61 observed, 18.2 expected and O/E 3.34.

The seasonal upper bars are less stable than the pooled result because
splitting the sample leaves few accidents in several cells. A tall
seasonal bar therefore needs to be read alongside its count. Gust is taken
from the same weather observation as mean wind, so agreement between the two
figures is complementary evidence about windy conditions rather than an
independent replication of the association.

\subsection{Temperature}

Figure~\ref{fig:temperature-oe-panels} shows a non-monotonic temperature pattern,
which requires a different interpretation from the upper-wind elevation.
Valid temperature was available for 6,259
accidents (97.58\%). Coverage by year is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:annual-coverage}, and no
temperature value is imputed. After adjustment by
temperature station and season, the estimates were non-monotonic. O/E was
below one at 3--6\(^{\circ}\)C for minor-injury accidents (632 observed and
881.7 expected; O/E 0.72), but above one at -3--0\(^{\circ}\)C. At
12\(^{\circ}\)C or above, 563 minor-injury accidents were observed compared
with 362.0 expected (O/E 1.56). This pattern may reflect travel
behaviour or weather and road conditions that vary with temperature. It is
not interpreted as a causal temperature effect.
For the serious-or-fatal group, all-year O/E is 0.69 at 3--6\(^{\circ}\)C
and 1.71 at 12\(^{\circ}\)C or above (176 and 226 accidents, respectively).

The pooled curve should not be read as a single temperature gradient:
temperature intervals occur with different frequencies across seasons,
and the rare seasonal extremes provide limited information. Standardising
within station and season accounts for those local frequencies, but does not
control traffic volume, road-surface condition or other circumstances that
vary within a season. The matched-time comparison below is therefore
particularly important for assessing whether the warm-end pattern persists.

\section{Matched-Time Results}

The case-crossover analysis includes 6,257 accident strata for each weather
measure. Table~\ref{tab:matched-time-summary} collects the main categorical
contrasts, leaving the text to explain their relationship to O/E.
For categorical mean wind, the clearest
increase occurs at 15 m/s or above: the odds ratio relative to 0--5 m/s is 1.61 (95\% CI
1.38--1.87). This supports the high-wind pattern after exact control for
station, year, month, weekday, and clock time. The lower and moderate wind
contrasts are near one. The persistence of the upper-wind pattern under these
controls makes an explanation based only on recurring calendar differences
less plausible, while leaving unusual traffic or road conditions on a
particular day uncontrolled.

\input{generated/matched_time_summary.tex}

For gust, the categorical estimates rise across the upper intervals and
agree with the upper-gust O/E pattern. The largest contrast is less precise
than those based on more common conditions. Because mean wind and gust
describe related aspects of the same observation, agreement does not
separate their individual contributions.

The temperature estimates are not monotonic. Relative to 0--3\(^{\circ}\)C,
the estimate is somewhat higher just below freezing, lower at
3--6\(^{\circ}\)C, and near one at 15\(^{\circ}\)C or above.
This last interval is narrower than the O/E
at-least-12\(^{\circ}\)C interval. The matched-time result reproduces the
dip at moderate temperatures but not the warm-tail increase in the O/E
analysis. The methods therefore give different results at warm temperatures,
which calls for caution.

When mean wind and temperature are included together, the matched odds ratio
for mean wind of at least 15 versus 0--5 m/s is 1.68 (95\% CI 1.44--1.96).
This is close to the single-variable estimate of 1.61, and the non-monotonic
temperature pattern remains.

\section{Daylight, Season, and Severity}

The matched-date daylight comparison holds location, month, weekday, and clock
time fixed. Relative to daylight, the odds ratio is 1.14 (95\% CI 0.88--1.49)
for darkness and 1.10 (0.92--1.31) for civil twilight. Only 985 of the 6,414
strata change daylight class within their matched dates. The result provides
no clear evidence of a daylight association and is much less
informative than the wind analysis; it does not use vehicle counts. Most
matched sets remain in the same daylight category and therefore cannot
distinguish the categories. This limited within-set variation, together with
intervals spanning one, is a reason for caution rather than evidence that
daylight is irrelevant to road safety.

Among the 6,259 accidents in the adjusted severity model, 1,424 are serious or
fatal. Mean wind of at least 15 m/s has an odds ratio of 0.93 (95\% CI
0.69--1.26) relative to 0--10 m/s. Darkness has an odds ratio of 0.89
(0.71--1.12) relative to daylight. Thus the data show a clear strong-wind
pattern for accident occurrence, but not for severity conditional on a
recorded injury accident. The complete adjusted estimates are shown in
Table~\ref{tab:severity-conditions} in the appendix.

The matched-time wind-by-season comparison uses the same 6,257
mean-wind strata as the case-crossover analysis, with coarse intervals for
stability. The omnibus interaction test gives
\(\chi^2(6)=14.30\), \(p=0.026\). The estimated odds ratios for at least
15 versus 0--10 m/s are 1.44 in winter, 2.02 in spring, 2.53 in summer, and
1.37 in autumn. The omnibus test provides evidence that at least some seasonal
wind associations differ, but it does not identify a particular pair of
seasons as different. Season-specific estimates are therefore not ranked.

\subsection{Daily-Counter Checks}

Observed daily traffic falls as strong wind persists. Relative to the calendar
expectation at the same counter, traffic is 86.8\% on days with at least six
hours at \(f\geq15\) m/s. This shows that traffic volume is not constant
across weather conditions at the selected counters. It does not quantify
how traffic changed across the full primary accident sample, because the
counters are geographically selective and do not provide complete hourly
traffic at accident times. The finding motivates the exposure checks rather
than supplying a numerical correction to primary O/E.

The allocated daily-counter model retains 762 accidents. Its rate ratio for at
least 15 versus 0--10 m/s is 3.62 (95\% CI 2.65--4.94), based on 47 accidents
in the upper interval. A counter-section calculation includes rural road
length and actual same-day weather for 694 daytime accidents. Its upper
mean-wind interval contains only eleven accidents. Both calculations start from
observed daily totals but estimate their within-day distribution, and both are
limited to selected counter locations. They are therefore supporting checks,
not the primary result.

The two daily calculations also ask different exposure questions: one fits
relative rates using allocated counter totals, while the same-day calculation
includes assigned road length and reports accidents per estimated VKT.
Their agreement in direction is useful, but cannot remove the uncertainty
in the within-day allocation or the sparse upper-wind counts.

\chapter{Discussion}

\section{Interpretation}

The primary O/E analysis shows that strong mean wind is over-represented at
the times of rural injury accidents relative to its local frequency. The
matched-time analysis supports this pattern after controlling for station and
recurring calendar time. The traffic results point in the same direction but
come from smaller samples and estimated exposure. The evidence therefore
supports an association, not a causal effect or a national per-vehicle risk.

Observed daily traffic is lower when strong wind persists at the selected
counters. This cautions against treating weather time as a direct measure of
vehicle exposure, but cannot establish how traffic contributed to the
primary O/E pattern across the wider network. Neither traffic source observes
traffic at the accident minute: the annual analysis allocates seasonal traffic
by wind frequency, and the daily analyses allocate observed totals within the
day. These results support rather than replace the primary comparison.

Gust also has an upper-tail pattern, although it is closely related to mean
wind. Temperature is non-monotonic and differs at the warm end between the O/E
and matched-time analyses. The daylight comparison gives no clear association,
and the severity model gives no clear evidence that strong wind increases
severity among recorded injury accidents. Sparse seasonal upper intervals do
not support ranking seasons or outcome groups.

The main result agrees with earlier studies comparing accident
shares with the time spent in adverse weather \citep{edwards1994} and with the
engineering evidence that crosswind can impair vehicle stability
\citep{baker1986,baker1994risk}. This study adds a longer Icelandic series,
local station-and-season standardisation, and matched-time and traffic checks;
it does not establish a new physical wind threshold.
The non-monotonic temperature estimates are consistent with previous evidence
that temperature associations can vary by range and season
\citep{lee2014,zhan2020}. The decrease in daily traffic during sustained wind is
consistent with recorder-based evidence that adverse weather changes travel
demand \citep{sathiaraj2018}.

\section{Strengths}

The study combines a long accident series with 10-minute weather observations,
local weather-frequency standardisation, and explicit reporting of match
coverage and observed counts. The matched-time and traffic analyses address
different weaknesses of the primary comparison. Documented transformations,
exclusions, and automated checks make the reported results reproducible.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item The main result is not traffic-adjusted. It should not be interpreted
  as accident risk per vehicle-kilometre.
  \item The case-crossover analysis controls recurring calendar time but not
  unusual traffic, road conditions, visibility, or closures on a particular
  day. Its $\geq15$ m/s category summarises the upper-wind
  pattern rather than a physical threshold.
  \item A weather station up to 20 km away is a proxy for conditions at the
  accident location. Terrain can produce important differences over shorter
  distances.
  \item The annual traffic analysis allocates seasonal traffic across wind
  intervals and does not observe traffic at the accident minute.
  \item Daily counters cover selected sites in 2019--2024 and provide no
  hourly traffic. Their totals are allocated within the day, and an assigned
  counter need not have been passed by the accident vehicle.
  \item Estimated rates are restricted to rural injury accidents on linked
  road sections. They should not be compared directly with broad national or
  international accident rates unless the outcome and vehicle-kilometre
  traffic quantity are defined in the same way.
  \item The highest wind intervals contain relatively few accidents. Several
  intervals and comparison results are shown, so individual estimates in
  the upper tail should be interpreted with caution.
  \item Fixed 2020--2024 urban boundaries are applied to the entire 2007--2025
  accident period.
  \item The analysis is observational. Unmeasured road condition, vehicle type,
  speed, visibility, and driver behaviour can contribute to the pattern.
\end{itemize}

\section{Future Work}

The most valuable addition would be verified hourly traffic with counter
coordinates, road-section identifiers, direction information, and documented
downtime. Linked to the same weather stations, these data could distinguish
vehicle classes and wind direction relative to the road. Daily totals cannot
provide that information.

\chapter{Conclusion}

Of 6,414 rural injury accidents during 2007--2025, 6,259 were matched to valid
weather observations within 20 km and five minutes. At mean wind speeds of at
least 20 m/s, 68 injury accidents occurred compared with 31.6 expected from
local weather frequency (O/E 2.15). The serious-or-fatal subset had O/E 1.87,
but its smaller count limits comparison between severity groups.

The matched-time analysis supports the upper-wind pattern: the odds ratio for
at least 15 m/s versus 0--5 m/s was 1.61. Gust gives a similar upper-tail
pattern, whereas the non-monotonic temperature estimates differ between the
O/E and matched-time analyses at warmer temperatures. The temperature finding
therefore remains uncertain.

Annual and daily traffic analyses support the high-wind pattern, and observed
daily traffic is lower during sustained strong wind.
These analyses cover narrower, selected samples and estimate traffic within
wind intervals from seasonal or daily totals; they do not provide complete
hourly exposure at accident times.

The analyses therefore show that rural injury accidents are relatively more
frequent during strong mean wind in Iceland. This is not a causal estimate or a complete
national measure of accident risk per vehicle. Such a measure would require
linked hourly traffic data with wider geographic coverage.
